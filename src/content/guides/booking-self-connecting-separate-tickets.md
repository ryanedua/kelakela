The flight search shows a through fare of six hundred dollars. You notice you could fly a budget carrier to the hub for ninety, then pick up a separate ticket onward for two hundred. Two hundred and ninety total. The saving looks obvious.

It might be. But the two options are not the same product, and the price gap is buying you something specific.

## What the extra money on a single ticket buys

When both legs sit on one ticket, the airline that sold it has taken responsibility for delivering you to your final destination. If the first leg runs late and you miss the second, that is their problem to solve. They rebook you, at no charge, on whatever is available — including, on many routes, a competitor's flight.

When you book the legs separately, you have taken that responsibility yourself. The second airline has no contract with you covering the first flight. It never heard of it. You are simply a passenger who did not show up, and no-shows do not get rebooked.

The through fare is not a rip-off. It is partly an insurance premium.

## Doing the actual arithmetic

The question is not "how much do I save." It is "how much do I save, weighted against what a failure costs."

A failure costs a **walk-up fare** on the second leg. Not the two hundred you paid — the price of that seat today, bought at the airport, hours before departure. On a busy route that is routinely three to five times the advance price. Sometimes there is no seat at all and you are buying tomorrow's flight plus a hotel.

So the honest comparison for the example above is not three hundred and ten dollars saved. It is three hundred and ten dollars saved against, say, a six hundred dollar replacement fare if things go wrong. The saving is real if the failure is rare enough. It stops being real the moment you are self-connecting through somewhere with weather, in the last bank of the evening, with no later flight to fall back on.

## What actually makes it go wrong

The instinct is to leave enough time to walk between gates. That is not the risk. Nobody misses a self-connection because they walked slowly.

The risk is that the **first flight is late at all** — and the amount it can be late by is unbounded. A ninety-minute delay eats a two-hour buffer. A cancelled first leg eats any buffer you care to build.

Which means the useful questions are about the first leg, not the layover:

- Is it the **last flight of the day** on that route? If the connection breaks, you sleep at the airport.
- Is it a **known-difficult airport** in a difficult season? Summer thunderstorms in the southeast, winter in the northeast.
- Is there a **later flight on the second leg** you could buy onto? If the answer is no, the downside is not a fare, it is a whole day.
- Are you connecting to a **cruise, a wedding, or the only flight to somewhere small**? Then the cost of failure isn't money at all.

If you must have a number: four hours domestic-to-domestic is a floor, not a comfort. Anything involving an international arrival — immigration, baggage reclaim, security again — needs a good deal more, and an overnight is not paranoid.

## Your bags do not follow you

On separate tickets the bags are checked only to the end of the first ticket. You collect them, walk them to the other terminal, and check them in again with the second airline — paying that airline's bag fee a second time.

That fee is part of the saving calculation and people routinely forget it. Two carriers at forty-five dollars a bag each way turns a cheap itinerary into a less cheap one.

Some airlines hold **interline agreements** that let them through-check bags across separate tickets on particular routes. Whether one applies to you is not something you can determine reliably at the counter, and agents disagree. Do not build a plan on it.

## How to tell what you have actually booked

The distinction is not always obvious, especially through online travel agencies that display both the same way.

**One ticket:** one record locator, one ticket number, one charge on your card. The itinerary reads as a single journey.

**Two tickets:** two confirmation emails, two record locators, two charges. Even if you booked them ninety seconds apart on the same site.

A **codeshare** is one ticket, despite involving two airlines. It carries a single record locator, and the airline whose flight number is on the ticket owns the connection. That is the opposite of what you are doing here.

If you are unsure, count the confirmation codes. That is the whole test.

## When it is a perfectly sensible thing to do

None of this means never do it. Self-connecting is reasonable when:

- The layover is **long enough to absorb a bad delay** — think overnight, or a deliberate stopover you would have wanted anyway.
- There are **several later flights** on the second leg, so a failure costs a fare and some hours rather than the trip.
- The saving is **large enough to fund a replacement ticket** and still be ahead.
- Your first leg goes somewhere with **redundancy** — a hub with hourly departures, not a once-a-day route.

The trip that fails is not usually the one with a tight connection. It is the one with no plan B.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know if my flights are on one ticket or two?**

Count the confirmation codes. One ticket means one record locator, one ticket number, one charge on your card. Two separate bookings mean two of everything, even if you made them minutes apart on the same website.

**How much layover do I need when self-connecting?**

There is no safe number, because the risk is not the walking time. It is the chance the first flight is late at all. Domestic to domestic, four hours is a reasonable floor. Anything involving an international arrival, immigration, or a bag recheck needs considerably more.

**Will my bags transfer between separate tickets?**

Generally no. You collect them at the connecting airport and check them in again with the second airline, paying that airline's bag fees a second time. Some carriers interline on specific routes, but you cannot rely on it.

**Is a codeshare the same as two separate tickets?**

No. A codeshare is one ticket sold under one airline's flight number but operated by another. It carries a single record locator and the selling airline is responsible for your connection. That is the opposite of self-connecting.

**Does travel insurance cover a missed self-connection?**

Sometimes, and it is worth reading the policy before you rely on it. Many trip delay benefits require a minimum delay length and exclude connections you arranged yourself. A credit card's built-in coverage often has the same carve-out.
