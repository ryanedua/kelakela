In 2020 the major US airlines dropped change fees, and they have advertised it ever since. The claim is true. It is also, on its own, close to useless — because the fee was rarely the expensive part.

## Two charges, not one

Changing a flight has always involved two separate amounts.

The **change fee** was a penalty. A flat sum, typically two hundred dollars domestic, that the airline took for the inconvenience of you existing. That is the one that went away.

The **fare difference** is the gap between what you paid and what your new flight costs today. That never went anywhere, and it was always the larger number on any route worth flying.

Move a two hundred dollar ticket to a Friday where the same seat now sells for three hundred and fifty, and you owe a hundred and fifty dollars. No penalty. Not free either.

## Why the gap is usually worse than the old fee

Airlines price by demand, and the day you need to move to is frequently the day everyone else wants too. Same route, Tuesday to Friday, and the difference can run well past what the old two hundred dollar penalty cost.

There is a second twist. The fare difference is calculated against **today's price for your new flight**, not against some average. If you are rebooking inside two weeks of departure — which is when most people rebook — you are buying at the walk-up end of the curve. The airline is not being punitive. It is simply selling you a last-minute ticket and crediting what you already paid.

## The exclusion nobody puts on the banner

Basic economy is carved out at every legacy carrier. Delta, United, American, Alaska: the no-change-fee policy covers Main Cabin and above, and stops there.

American is the strictest of them. Basic economy on American cannot be changed at all once the 24-hour window closes. Not for a fee — at all. Your options are to cancel inside 24 hours, or to forfeit the ticket and buy a new one.

This is the single most common reason people feel lied to. The airline said no change fees, the airline meant it, and the fare they sold you was never covered.

## Southwest is no longer the exception

For decades the honest answer to "which airline lets me change anything" was Southwest. That stopped being true on **27 January 2026**.

Southwest rebuilt its fare structure into four tiers — Basic, Choice, Choice Preferred, Choice Extra — and the new Basic fare **cannot be changed**. To capture a lower price or move your flight on a Basic ticket, you cancel outright and take a credit, and that credit expires in six months and is not transferable.

Choice and above still carry no change fee, and on those fares Southwest remains genuinely more flexible than its rivals: unlimited re-fares, twelve-month transferable credits.

But if you read somewhere that Southwest has no change fees on anything, you are reading something written before January. It is a good illustration of why a fact about airline policy is worth very little without a date attached to it.

## The budget carriers never joined

Spirit, Frontier and Allegiant still charge a change penalty, generally between sixty-nine and a hundred and nineteen dollars, and you pay the fare difference on top.

The no-change-fee shift was a legacy carrier decision made under pandemic conditions to compete for nervous bookings. The ultra-low-cost carriers were never in that fight.

## When the new flight is cheaper

Sometimes the fare moves your way. What happens next varies, and it is worth knowing before you rebook rather than after.

Some carriers issue a travel credit for the difference. Others keep it. None of them give you cash back. And the credit will carry an expiry — often twelve months from the **original ticket issue date**, not from the date you cancelled, which quietly shortens it by however long you held the booking.

## What to actually check before you book

If there is any chance you will move the flight, the useful questions are not about change fees:

- Is this fare **basic economy** or its equivalent? If yes, assume you cannot change it.
- If the new flight is cheaper, do I get the difference back, and in what form?
- If I take a credit, **when does it expire, and from what date is that measured**?
- Is this an international route originating outside North or South America? Some carriers still charge a change fee on those, and it can reach four hundred dollars.

The gap between a basic economy fare and the next tier up is frequently smaller than the cost of one change. That is not an accident of pricing.

## What the DOT rule does and does not cover

None of the above is affected by the federal refund rules. Those cover situations where the **airline** cancels or significantly changes your flight — then you are owed an automatic cash refund if you decline the alternative.

Voluntary changes, the kind covered here, are governed entirely by the fare rules you agreed to at booking. There is no federal protection when you are the one moving the date.

## Frequently asked questions

**If there is no change fee, why am I being asked for money?**

Because the fee and the fare difference are two separate charges. Airlines removed the penalty and kept the difference. If your new flight costs more than the original, you pay the gap, and on a popular route that gap is often larger than the old fee ever was.

**Does no change fee apply to basic economy?**

No. Basic economy is excluded at Delta, United, American, and Alaska. American does not permit changes on it at all after the 24-hour window. This is the single most common reason people believe they were misled.

**Is Southwest still the airline with no change fees on everything?**

Not since 27 January 2026. Southwest rebuilt its fares into Basic, Choice, Choice Preferred and Choice Extra, and the new Basic fare cannot be changed. Choice and above still carry no change fee. A great deal of published advice has not caught up with this.

**What happens if my new flight is cheaper than the original?**

It depends on the carrier and fare. Some issue a travel credit for the difference; others keep it. Nobody hands back cash. Check before you rebook, because the credit usually carries an expiry you were not told about.

**Do budget airlines charge change fees?**

Yes. Spirit, Frontier and Allegiant still charge a change penalty, generally in the 69 to 119 dollar range, on top of any fare difference. The no-change-fee shift was a legacy carrier move and the ultra-low-cost carriers did not follow.
