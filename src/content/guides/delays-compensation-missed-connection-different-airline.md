The answer turns on one thing almost nobody checks before booking: whether your two flights are on **one ticket** or **two**.

That single distinction decides whether the second airline rebooks you for free or shrugs and sells you a walk-up fare. It is not about which airlines they are, how long the layover was, or whether the delay was obviously not your fault.

## One ticket, two airlines

If both segments share a record locator and a single ticket number, you have one contract of carriage. The airline that sold you the ticket owes you a rebooking to your destination, at no charge, even if a partner carrier operated the leg that ran late. This covers most codeshares and most itineraries booked in a single transaction through an airline or an online travel agency.

Go to the second airline's counter, not the first one's. The delay is the first airline's fault, but the obligation to get you there belongs to whoever's ticket you are holding.

## Two tickets, no relationship

If you booked the legs separately — two confirmations, two ticket numbers, two credit card charges — the second airline has no contract with you for the first flight. You are a no-show. They are not obligated to rebook you, and in practice they usually won't do it for free.

This is the case that catches people out on cheap self-connected itineraries. The savings on the separate booking evaporate the first time a delay eats the connection.

## Two tickets, but the airlines interline

There is a middle case. Some carriers hold **interline agreements** that let them hand passengers and bags to each other even across separate tickets, on specific routes. Whether one applies to you is not something you can reliably determine at the gate, and agents differ on how they read it.

If you are in this situation, ask specifically: *"Do you interline with [carrier] on this route?"* That phrasing gets a real answer. "Can you help me?" does not.

## What to do in the moment

Ask the delaying airline for written confirmation of the delay and its cause before you leave the gate area. A screenshot of the flight status page with a timestamp works. If you later have any claim at all — against your ticketing airline, a credit card's trip delay benefit, or travel insurance — that evidence is what the claim turns on, and it becomes much harder to obtain a week later.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will an airline rebook me if my delayed inbound flight was on a different carrier?**

Only if the two flights are on one ticket, or the carriers hold an interline agreement that covers the route. On genuinely separate tickets bought in separate transactions, the second airline treats you as a no-show and owes you nothing.

**Does a codeshare count as one ticket?**

Usually yes. If both segments carry a single record locator and one ticket number, the marketing carrier is responsible for rebooking you even though a different airline operated the delayed leg.

**Can I claim compensation from the airline that caused the delay?**

In the US, no. There is no federal rule requiring compensation for delays, and the delaying carrier has no contract with you for a flight it did not sell you. Your claim, if any, is against the airline that sold your ticket.
